Most camera apps give you the picture instantly and a hundred filters to pick from. Darkroom does the opposite: you pick an old film or an old camera, take the shot, and live with how it turns out. Film has to develop. Digital shots sit on a memory card until you copy them off. It's meant to make taking pictures feel a bit more like it used to.

FILM MODE
• Five films: a clean, punchy colour film, a soft and warm portrait film, a gritty black & white film, square instant prints in 8-shot packs, and a Super 8 movie camera.
• Every shot goes to the darkroom and takes about five minutes to develop (instant prints fade in over 20 seconds). A quiet notification tells you when it's ready, even if the app is closed.
• Developed prints get pinned to a corkboard. Each frame has real film grain and the odd speck of dust, hair or light leak, tuned against scans of real film.
• Write a note on the border of an instant print.
• Super 8 reels play on a projector with chunky tape-deck keys, fast forward and rewind that actually spool, and a label you can rename.
• Nothing goes to your gallery until you tap Save.

DIGITAL MODE
• Four cameras: a 1999 floppy-disk camera, a 2003 CCD compact, a Y2K flip phone and a 90s camcorder with an optional tape display burned into the video.
• Shots land on a pretend SD card, browsed in a Windows 98-style file explorer. The camcorder writes to floppy disks, so mind the disk swaps.
• Files are locked on the card until you move them to the C: drive, which also copies them to your gallery.
• There are a few hidden things in the explorer's menus.

Swap between film and digital by tossing the camera off the desk with your thumb.

No accounts, no ads, no tracking. Your photos stay on your phone until you save or share them.
